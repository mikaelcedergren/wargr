# The approved reference in practice

The author approved the complete Babylon reading draft on 27 October 2026 and called it “2000%
better”. Its exact text lives once in [the compiled voice reference](../../server/src/voice-reference.ts).
That entire piece is supplied to every polish request. Read it consecutively before studying these
excerpts: isolated strong sentences do not establish pacing or voice.

The author subsequently refined its pacing. Those later directions and his separate four-paragraph
example, described below, take precedence over treating the accepted Babylon edition's sentence
breaks as a finished rhythm model. The historical edition remains unchanged.

The following observations explain choices in an approved piece. They are editorial analysis,
not separate claims that the author approved each technique, and they are not sentence templates.
The [canonical contract](../../server/src/voice-contract.ts) remains the instruction authority.

## The author's later pacing refinement

After accepting Babylon, Michael objected to repeated sentence-period-sentence progression. He
asked for the variation illustrated by Gary Provost, with more room for the long sentences to
develop: take a natural opening to make a sentence longer, and skip the join if it has to be forced.
He then supplied the exact four-paragraph opening preserved as `AUTHOR_PACING_REFERENCE` in
[voice-reference.ts](../../server/src/voice-reference.ts). It is included in every polish request.
The full passage is held there once, with its paragraph breaks intact.

His further clarification supplies the governing principle: pacing depends on understanding what
the piece says and where the story is about to lead. Sentence and paragraph lengths do not create
that understanding. First follow the whole movement, including what the reader knows, feels or
expects; then choose whether to carry them forward or give them a pause before the next passage.

In his four-paragraph example, the opening recollection is allowed to stand alone. The next
paragraph accumulates the behaviour behind it in one developing sentence. A shorter paragraph
then holds attention on repeating headlines, before the fourth moves from another recognisable
act into the concern about belonging. The topic has not changed at every break. The pauses change
how the thought arrives and where attention rests. These are editorial observations about the
author's example, not a requirement to repeat its four-part shape or endorse its claims elsewhere.

The following two passages are editorial illustrations based on his rhythm feedback, with grammar
cleaned up. They are not new complete author-approved drafts and do not replace the historical
Babylon reference:

> I don't find the figure of the false prophet particularly antiquated when I read it this way.
> A person who can give cruelty a respectable purpose has something valuable to offer because
> they spare others the discomfort of wanting it for themselves.

The cause and its consequence belong together. Connecting them lets the thought develop without
turning its last clause into a separate pronouncement. The join carries meaning; it does not add
material merely to make the sentence longer.

> In Stanley Milgram's obedience experiment, published in 1963, volunteers were told to administer
> increasingly severe electric shocks as part of a study of learning. The recipient was acting
> and received no shocks, while the volunteers were being asked to proceed as though the shocks
> were real. Twenty-six of forty continued to the highest setting. Fourteen stopped.

The second sentence keeps the difference between the setup and the participants' instructions
together. The two short results then have space to register. This is a local decision about the
explanation and its consequence, not a pattern of long-long-short-short to impose on other material.

[Purdue Global's Writing Center](https://purdueglobalwriting.center/writing-powerful-sentences-2/)
uses Provost to explain how sentence variety avoids monotony and helps create rhythm. The more
specific preference for taking naturally longer openings is Michael's direction, not a claim
that Provost prescribes Wargr's style. Neither source justifies comma splices, padded sentences,
uniform paragraph blocks, or isolating every observation for emphasis.

## A point of view with something specific to notice

> I was especially suspicious of how often God's wishes suited the men speaking on his behalf.

The narrator's scepticism concerns an identifiable relationship between authority and benefit.
He has an opinion, and the reader can understand what gives rise to it. The sentence does not
announce a revelation or describe his attitude as profound. A different subject needs a different
observation; repeating this sentence's grammar would not preserve its value.

## Humour that belongs to the subject

> The book does not make much effort to accommodate the reader who would prefer a tasteful metaphor.

The preceding sentences have shown Revelation's beast, jewels and cup. The amusement follows from
that exuberant imagery meeting the narrator's former expectations. It does not depend on making a
believer stupid or appending a joke unrelated to the thought. This works because the reader has
already encountered what is funny.

## Exact consequences carrying emotional weight

> People are being sold, and the men watching the city burn are mourning the market.

The paragraph has first established which city, which merchants and what their inventory contains,
with a source. The last sentence brings two facts together. Its force depends on those particulars;
turning it into a general maxim about greed would lose the discrepancy the reader can see.

## A developed illustration rather than a list of settings

The fundraiser is introduced with “Suppose” and follows one proposal: publicising the names of
families who have not contributed. An objection changes the conversation from those families'
circumstances to the objector's own effort. The example lets the reader follow the change of subject
and the permission it provides. It does not need a second hypothetical office, political movement
or family dinner to make the same point.

It is explicitly fictional reasoning, not a newly invented incident in Michael's life. The
publication of names is a specific consequential act; generic discussion of “the room” would not
do the same work.

## Self-implication without inventing an experience

> I would want him to take responsibility. But I would still be the person operating the switches.

The surrounding paragraph is openly imagined, after a description of Milgram's study. The narrator
does not claim he took part or knows how he would behave. He considers the attraction of passing
responsibility upward while acknowledging his remaining agency. This supplies personal stakes
without fabricating a memory or declaring that every reader would obey.

## An ending supported by the whole piece

The ending returns to the merchants' understandable grief and to the people who were already
suffering while trade continued. The narrator's wish to have cared earlier is narrower and more
uncomfortable than a general declaration that everyone is corrupt. It leaves the reader with an
actual question about attention and benefit, not an instruction about what to feel.

The return is useful in this piece because the intervening examples change how the merchants can be
read. It is not a requirement that every Wargr story close with its opening image. Other material
may finish with a plain conclusion, an action, an unresolved consequence, or an observation.

## The ingress invites the reader in

After the first corpus rewrite, Michael rejected the introductions under the story titles and
clarified their sole purpose: make the reader curious enough to read the whole article. They do
not have to settle anything on their own, and may finish with an unfinished thought or “...”.
The canonical contract's introduction section records this decision for future writing.

The rejected introduction to Knowing Yourself read:

> At eighteen, I asked my sister whether I was ugly. Her answer lasted a few seconds. I kept
> finding uses for it over the next twenty-five years.

Its few-seconds/twenty-five-years arrangement supplies conspicuous emphasis, while “finding uses”
leaves the reader guessing what might be interesting. Replacing it with a tidy explanation of
self-image would misunderstand the author's correction. The task is to choose an intriguing
entrance into this actual story and leave the reader wanting to follow it. An unanswered question
or withheld outcome is allowed; the reader needs a reason to care about finding it out.

The revised candidate applies that distinction to the actual conversation:

> I asked my sister whether I was ugly, hoping for a little reassurance, and her answer began
> well enough: “You’re not ugly...”

This ends inside the answer, leaving the reader wanting to hear the rest. The article supplies it.
It illustrates the author's instruction; it has not separately been accepted as a new approved
voice exemplar. Other stories may invite curiosity without dialogue, a personal opening or an
ellipsis.

Read the title, ingress and subsequent article as a sequence. The title already supplies context;
the introduction can develop curiosity rather than repeat it. Then check that the article fulfils
the invitation. Evaluate that relationship and the prose, without requiring a miniature essay,
adding an ellipsis to every item, or mistaking an internal review pass for the author's approval.

## Whole-story context in a line edit

The author explicitly clarified that the ghostwriter must read and assess an article as a whole
story even when the requested work is grammar correction. The canonical contract's **Read the
whole story before editing** section owns this instruction for all modes. The following invented
example illustrates the distinction; it is not an approved voice reference or biographical material:

> I thought he had forgotten me.
>
> Every Thursday, a letter arrived. I left them unopened.
>
> It took me months to admit that I was the one refusing to answer.

The opening belief and the letters appear to contradict each other when read as isolated factual
statements. The ending makes that tension intelligible: the narrator is describing an earlier
belief and later recognising their own refusal. Rewriting the opening to say the narrator knew
they had not been forgotten would remove the movement of the story. A grammar pass elsewhere
does not authorise resolving that tension or explaining the ending sooner.

The same care applies to a small connective edit. **Because** gives a reason; **but** introduces a
contrast. Either may produce a fluent sentence while changing the author's explanation. The
surrounding scene and the rest of the article must support the choice. If they do not settle it,
fluency alone is insufficient evidence to make that change.

This is a reading and judgement requirement within the existing full-document request. It does
not introduce another provider call or claim that a mechanical check can establish understanding.

## What not to copy

Do not reuse the religious imagery, study, school example, merchant ending, or personal statements
as facts or stock devices for other stories. Do not aim for the same paragraph count or sequence.
The approved direction is visible in the exact observations, intelligible reasoning, willingness
to implicate the narrator, and sustained attention to consequences. The next piece has to find
those qualities in its own material.
